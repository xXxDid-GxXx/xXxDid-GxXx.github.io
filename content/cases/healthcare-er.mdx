## What this actually is (intro)

I'm the AI product owner on this project, and it's still running as I write this.

The client is a US emergency room network. Their patient intake and physician referrals lived in Google Forms, Power Apps and paper. They wanted ==a real product that talks to their EMR==, cuts manual data entry, and stops losing information between the front desk and the physicians.

Two flows: patient intake, for people arriving at the ER or pre-registering online, and physician referral, for doctors at other clinics sending patients in.

## The team

![Front desk patient queue — every patient from expected to discharge](/images/cases/healthcare-er/front-desk-queue.png)

Three people.

Me as product owner: client-facing, running discovery, presentations and design. In practice also the analyst and the design lead. An architect running the engineering strategy and the integration design. A developer building the actual product.

That's the whole team, on a healthcare product for a US client.

My company calls this a self-contained unit. ==Three senior people with the autonomy to move fast==, without the layers of PMs and leads that slow enterprise consulting down. It works because nobody needs a committee to make a decision, and because AI does the work that used to need a bigger team.

I got the seat through the company's AI initiative. When the healthcare vertical needed a small unit, a few of us got pulled in.

## What was there before

![Adding a walk-in — scan the ID to prefill instead of retyping](/images/cases/healthcare-er/add-patient.png)

Google Forms, Power Apps, and some very creative Excel.

The old flow: a patient fills out a form, on paper or a tablet. A PSA at the front desk reads it, opens the EMR in another window, and ==retypes every field by hand==. Name, date of birth, insurance, symptoms, medications. One field at a time.

That's where the mistakes come from. Typos in insurance numbers. Skipped fields. Names spelled differently across systems, creating duplicate records. Referrals from outside clinics arriving as PDFs, faxes, or on bad days, phone calls.

None of this is unusual. It's how most emergency rooms run. The tech behind the counter is decades behind the CT scanner ten feet away. Our job was to close that gap.

## What we're building

![Patient pre-registration — the About You step with a QR code to continue on your phone](/images/cases/healthcare-er/intake-about-you.png)

Two flows, several entry points, several roles. The shape of it:

On the patient side, pre-registration online before you leave the house. ==OCR for insurance cards and IDs==, so nobody types twenty digits into a form. Voice input for the reason for your visit, because if you're going to the ER you're probably in no state to type. Geolocation check-in: once you're near the clinic, you tap a button and you're in. No queue at the front desk behind the family whose kid just threw up.

## Every role gets its own screen

![Check-in by ID scan — confirm the details read from the license](/images/cases/healthcare-er/scan-check-in.png)

Referring physicians get a structured referral form. ==A proper record goes into the ER's system instead of a fax.==

Front desk staff get a dashboard with every patient in the pipeline: pre-registered, en route, arrived, checked in. It's also the bridge to the EMR, more on that next.

ER physicians get the full patient context, so by the time someone reaches an exam room nobody is reading a printed Google Form.

The client's business team gets analytics: where patients drop off, how long each stage takes, which entry points bring the most volume.

Each of these is its own application, shipping in phases.

## The bridge to the EMR

![Patient detail — registration readiness and Send to Paster](/images/cases/healthcare-er/patient-detail.png)

This is ==the smartest thing we've shipped on this project==.

The endgame is a direct API integration with the client's EMR. But getting API access from enterprise EMR vendors takes weeks of paperwork and security reviews, and the client's security team wasn't ready to open that door. With patient records at stake, I don't blame them. We couldn't wait, and we shouldn't have pushed.

So we built a bridge. The first version was simple: a PSA clicks a button on our dashboard, and the patient's data appears in a small floating window that stays on top of everything. They open the EMR next to it and paste each field across. ==Two clicks per field instead of thirty keystrokes.==

## Then we made it smarter

![Visit information laid out in the EMR's own fields and order](/images/cases/healthcare-er/visit-info.png)

We mapped the EMR's forms: which field comes first, where each Tab lands, the whole keyboard sequence a PSA walks through by hand. Then we built a small automation around that map. The PSA opens the EMR, clicks into the first field, presses one button in our app, and the app types it all in. Paste, Tab, paste, Tab, through every field in order.

In effect, ==we built a bot that pretends to be a PSA typing==. To the EMR it's just keyboard input. No API, no integration, no security review. To the PSA, patient data moves across with two button presses.

It isn't elegant, but it ships now, solves the real pain, and the security team is fine with it. That bridge is what convinced the client the rest of the project would work.

## What's hard about this

![Discharge with the full set of dispositions](/images/cases/healthcare-er/discharge.png)

The team structure. With three people and no formal analyst, design lead or PM, those roles land on somebody. Here, mostly on me.

The first weeks were uncomfortable. Nobody was going to hand me a brief, tell me which artifacts to make, or run the client calls. I had to work out what the project needed and build the way to deliver it, from scratch, with the client watching.

This is where AI earns its keep. Every artifact I make, from journey maps and screen flows to requirements and client decks, starts as a conversation with Claude or Google AI Studio. I describe what I need, get a scaffold, sharpen it, ship it. Days of drafting become hours.

==Without AI, this project would need at least five more people.==

## Where we are

![Patient records — signed consents and uploaded documents with review status](/images/cases/healthcare-er/patient-records.png)

==The prototype was approved. The client bought it.==

The three of us found the problem, scoped it, prototyped it, proved it and sold it. The project is now in delivery: a larger team is building the production version, and I consult with them on every product decision. I've just finished the work breakdown and estimates with them.

From here my role shifts. Less building, more product decisions, more making sure delivery keeps the thinking that got us here.

Soon the front desk stops retyping patient records, and the ER routes patients with fewer errors than paper ever allowed. Three people with AI took it from Google Forms to an approved production prototype.

That's what I do now.
