# AI Orchestration

## Where this came from (intro)

I had just moved to a new team, and they didn't know me yet. Their previous projects were the kind every IT consultancy lives on: tables, dashboards, internal admin tools. The work that pays the rent and bores everyone involved.

I was two months in, and ==the team hadn't seen what I could do beyond the basics==. That's why this project mattered to me more than the brief required.

## The product

![Start a goal — describe what you want in plain English](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/start-goal.png)

A platform that lets non-technical people build automated workflows by talking to an AI.

It sounds like Make or n8n, but those tools make you drag boxes around a canvas, connect nodes, configure triggers and debug API calls. Powerful, and unreadable to anyone who isn't a developer.

Our pitch was simpler. You describe the workflow in plain English: "every time a candidate applies, screen their resume against the role, score them, and open a Slack thread for the hiring manager with the top three." The orchestrator breaks that into a chain of AI agents, configures and connects them, and runs it for you.

==The user never sees the wiring.== The target user was the HR person or the ops manager with fifteen repetitive tasks a week and no way to automate them.

## When this was

![Goals board — pending, in progress and finished goals](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/goals-board.png)

Early 2025. Most people still used AI for copywriting and one-off questions. Chaining agents together for real multi-step work was still future tense for most clients.

I had been using AI seriously since the day it launched. My girlfriend showed me ChatGPT the week it came out, and I understood right away that ==the rules of design work were about to change==. This project was the first time I got to design *for* AI users instead of just being one.

## The org problem

![The orchestrator asks clarifying questions before it plans](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/clarifying-questions.png)

Before the design problem, the organizational one. ==For most of its life the project had no single decision-maker.==

The other designer and I built mockups for weeks and got most of the screens done. The analyst, the sales team and various stakeholders all had opinions. None of them had the final say. We were designing by committee for a moving target, which means designing for nobody.

Then, late in the process, a real decision-maker appeared, with real overrides. Some of the work survived. Some got redesigned for reasons that had more to do with preferences and sales pressure than usability.

Design wanted the most useful product for the users we'd interviewed. Sales wanted an MVP that could start earning. Both fair. But without a referee every meeting became a debate about direction, and that eats momentum.

I learned a lot here about when to push and when to let go.

## Showing the workflow

![The plan as a list — agents and their steps, before the canvas](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/manager-plan.png)

The orchestrator generates workflows. Workflows are chains of agents. The question that ate most of our design time: how do you show the user what's happening?

We tried a plain list of steps first. "Step 1: parse resume, step 2: extract skills, step 3: score against role." A developer reads that and feels oriented. An HR coordinator reads it and feels handed a stack trace.

The next idea, pushed by the developers, was Kanban. Each agent gets a card, and cards move through queued, running, completed, failed.

==We pushed back, hard.== Real workflows here aren't linear. They branch, with three agents working at once and merging back. They loop. They go backwards when an agent needs more context. And agents finish in seconds, so cards would flicker between columns several times a second. The board would look like a slot machine.

## The canvas

![The visual workflow canvas — parallel agent branches merging into a final result](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/visual-workflow.png)

So we went the other way: a canvas. Each agent is a cell placed in space. Lines between them show what depends on what, what runs in parallel, what loops back. Colour shows state: idle, working, done, errored. Small notifications bloom over an agent as it reports progress and fade out again. The whole workflow becomes a living thing on a board.

Users got it immediately. ==The canvas made parallel work legible.== Three agents running side by side looked like three agents running side by side. You could glance at the board and know what the system was doing without reading a word.

The surprise was that people found it fun, in the watching-an-ant-colony way. They'd open the canvas just to watch their workflows run. We hadn't designed for that, and it became one of the product's strongest hooks.

## Don't make AI look human

![Pixel-art agent avatars](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/agent-avatars.png)

Early on we tried giving the orchestrator a human-like avatar: soft features, a friendly persona, a conversational tone. The theory was that a human face would build trust.

==Users hated it.== The feedback was consistent: a human-looking AI made them *less* willing to trust it. It felt uncanny, or it felt like the AI was pretending to be something it wasn't. They preferred AI that looked like AI.

So we pivoted. The orchestrator became a small pixelated shape that reads as "computer", not "almost a person". The whole product followed: pixel art, slightly retro, a look that says this is clever software, and it's not pretending to be your friend.

That finding became the product's identity. It's the same instinct as Glazd, inverted: be honest about what the thing is, and people reward you for it.

## Templates

![Capability explorer — a library of agents by role and skill](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/capability-explorer.png)

One of the smaller things we built: a library of ready-made workflow templates for each role and department. HR onboarding, marketing campaign automation, ops alert routing.

Not because users were lazy. Most people still don't know what AI can do, and they underestimate it constantly. Even now, most use it like a search engine. It rarely occurs to them to just ask whether something is possible. ==The limit on what people get out of AI is mostly imagination, not capability.==

Templates lowered that tax. You scroll the library, think "I didn't know you could do that," and your ceiling moves up a little. Half of what the templates did was make users braver.

## What this case actually is

![An agent profile with its tools, skills and ready-to-use prompts](/images/cases/ai-orchestration/agent-profile.png)

Not a triumph story. The organization made it harder than it needed to be, the decision-maker showed up late, and some of the best work got compromised.

But the design wins were real. We won the Kanban-versus-canvas argument. We acted on the research and stopped making AI look human. The template library quietly raised what users thought was possible. And the team I'd just joined saw I could do more than tables.

That last part mattered most. This project was ==the work that got me promoted from designer to product owner== a year or so later, because it showed who I was when given something real.

The product shipped and is in market. AI orchestration is a category now, with plenty of competitors. Looking at what they built, ==our take on the canvas holds up.==
