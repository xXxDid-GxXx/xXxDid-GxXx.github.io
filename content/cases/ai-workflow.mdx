## Before design

![On deck between watches — ship engineer, before design](/images/cases/ai-workflow/ship-deck.jpg)

==Before I was a designer, I was a ship engineer.==

Engine room, cargo vessels, back and forth across the planet. Every day I woke up to maintain engines and the dozens of mechanical systems that keep a ship from sinking. Real grease, hard hands, the kind of work where doing it badly means people drown.

It's the least glamorous origin story for a designer I can think of, which is why it's the right place to start. Most designers were always going to be designers. I wasn't. I got here by a different road, and the road shaped how I work.

## The ten months

![Sunset at sea, somewhere in the ten months](/images/cases/ai-workflow/ocean-sunset.jpg)

COVID hit while I was at sea. ==A voyage that should have taken three months took ten.==

People assume I left because the work was hard. It was, but it always was. I left because I couldn't see anyone I loved for ten months. There's a particular kind of maths you do at sea, counting what you earn against what you miss. Somewhere in those ten months the maths stopped working.

My girlfriend back home was a UI/UX designer. Between voyages I'd watch what she did. Figma, meetings, clients, deliverables. Honestly, ==I didn't believe her job was real==. I couldn't see how anyone paid money for what looked like rearranging rectangles.

Ten months of staring at the ocean is a long time to revisit a wrong assumption.

## ChatGPT arrives

Then ChatGPT launched. My girlfriend showed it to me, the way she'd shown me other software. This time it was different.

I could ask. In my own words, whatever was on my mind. Why does this work this way? What does this function do? Show me an example. I didn't have to read chapters in order. I could start anywhere, and it would meet me at my level and pull me up to the next one, until I'd accidentally learned something that would have taken six months of structured study.

This is the part people don't talk about. For those of us whose brains were never going to get through a textbook, it's the first tool that teaches the way we actually learn. ==It removed a barrier I'd accepted as permanent.==

## Why design, not development

I tried Figma not long after.

I make music, and I'd used FL Studio for years, a serious tool with a punishing interface. After that, Photoshop felt brutal and ==Figma felt obvious==. Everything was where I expected. Every tool did what it said.

That clicked something. If software could be this well designed, someone was designing it. Someone cared where every button lived. I suddenly understood what designers did, because I was using their work and feeling the difference.

I also looked at development again, more seriously. And I realised developers don't decide much. They build what someone else designed and specified. Real, hard, valuable work, but not where the decisions live.

==I wanted to be where the decisions live.== Eight months later I had my first design job.

## How I work now

Honestly, ==I don't really know how to work without AI.==

That isn't an exaggeration. From the day I started learning design, AI was in the workflow. Every project, every research phase, every moment of being stuck. I'd just ask.

I could work without it. I know the methods, the artifacts, the patterns. But the book method, find an article, read someone else's account of how to make a journey map, apply it, is slow and lossy. The chat method is direct. I describe what I'm trying to do, get back the relevant patterns and the right questions, and then I do the work.

==AI doesn't do my thinking. It does my remembering.== It holds everything good design has looked like; I bring the judgement about what applies here, for this team. The decisions and the accountability are mine.

That's how I ended up in my company's AI initiative, and a few months later, ==promoted to AI product owner==.

## The interesting part

Life keeps going. If AI eventually gets good enough to replace what I do, fine. I changed careers once already, from sailor to designer, in under a year, in a field I didn't believe was real until I was inside it. If I have to change again, I will.

The deeper thing, and the part I'd want a hiring manager to read, is that I don't think of myself as a designer first. I'm ==someone with a creative head who happens to be doing design right now==.

I made music for years. I shot music videos. I built things. The through-line isn't design. It's that I see things differently and can't stop making things. Design is problems with twenty plausible answers, and my job is finding the one that matters.

That's the part AI can't replace, because it isn't a skill. It's a way of being. ==Tools change. The way I look at problems doesn't.==

## The thesis

==The process is the product.==

Not a slogan, the actual claim. The Figma files are outputs. The work is the way I think through problems: using AI to compress research and ideation, and stacking pattern-matching from music, video, engines and design on top of each other when I sit down with something hard.

The tools will all be different next year. ==The process compounds.==

I became a designer by asking questions. The questions got me here, and they'll take me wherever I'm going next.

I'm not the guy who reads the books. ==I'm the guy who asks the questions.==
