# Linear Marketplace

## Where I was (intro)

Early in my career. I thought I understood design. ==I had no idea what I didn't know.==

The version of me on this project had the basics: visual hierarchy, components, decent Figma skills. What I hadn't learned yet was the thing that makes a senior designer, which is being wrong in interesting ways and noticing. A lot of that started here.

The work shipped and the case is real. I just wouldn't make all the same calls today, and that's how careers work.

## The brief (intro)

A crypto company wanted a marketplace for NFTs and tokens. Standard enough, until the second paragraph of the brief: the marketplace also had to support ==selling tokens whose smart contracts said they couldn't be transferred==.

Tokens often come with transfer restrictions written into the contract. The hook was that this marketplace would let you sell them anyway, through a peer-to-peer escrow trick the engineers had worked out.

I didn't understand the legality of that then, and I'm not sure I do now. What I understood was that the client wanted it built, and I had a year of experience to work out what it should look like.

## What I didn't know about crypto

![Connect Wallet modal over the marketplace](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/connect-wallet.png)

Everything. I didn't know how blockchains worked, what gas fees were, why a wallet shows three versions of the same coin, or why moving a token between chains means trusting a bridge that might eat your money.

I had to learn all of it just to draw the screens.

The main revelation: a token lives on one chain. If you sell it through a marketplace on another chain, you pay fees in that chain's currency, which you probably don't own. So you buy some first, which is another transaction and another fee. ==Someone selling a $20 NFT could realistically pay $15 in fees across three transactions.==

And the wallet shows all of this raw. Every fee, every chain, every gas estimate, in numbers nobody translates for you.

## Why crypto looks like a casino

![Calm NFT grid — browse view](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/nft-grid.png)

This is why every crypto product looks like a slot machine.

There's so much going on, in so many directions, that the only way to say "it's fine, trust us" is to add more lights and animation. The casino look isn't a style choice. It's a coping mechanism for hostile information density. When the data underneath makes no sense, you make the surface feel exciting. ==Excitement covers for confusion.==

The unspoken brief inside the real brief was to refuse that. To treat the user as someone who deserves to understand what's happening to their money.

## Big Solar

![Token grid with a plain-language risk warning tooltip](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/token-warning.png)

This didn't happen in isolation. My company had an internal initiative called Big Solar, set up specifically to take on crypto work. The idea was that crypto products were universally badly designed, and a serious agency could make them feel like real software instead of casinos with extra steps.

I got pulled into several of those projects. Not all were glamorous. Some were pure UX audits: go screen by screen, mark every problem, score it, hand over the document.

That kind of work teaches you what trust looks like in a UI. Crypto users have lost real money to bad design, so "looks trustworthy" isn't a vibe here, ==it's a load-bearing feature==. Confirmation steps, plain-language summaries, warnings before irreversible actions. Banking apps had done all this for decades. Crypto had simply skipped that phase.

## The thing that changed me

![NFT list view — the same offers, structured as a table](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/list-view.png)

==Information architecture.==

It sounds dry. It was the most important thing that happened to me as a designer in those years.

Before this project, UX artifacts were things I made because the process said so. User flows, IA maps, audits: deliverables to prove the work was done before moving on to the "real" design.

The marketplace was too complicated to design on intuition. I couldn't open Figma and start drawing, because I didn't know what the screens needed to do or what belonged next to what. So I did the IA work for real. I sat with the client's reference marketplaces, mapped what they had and why it confused people, and diagrammed our own structure, several times, until each version was clearly better than the last.

## The artifacts are the work

![Token search filtered to UNI](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/token-search.png)

And it worked. By the time I opened Figma, half the decisions were already made, not because I'd guessed well, but because the diagrams had done their job.

That's when it clicked: ==UX artifacts aren't proof that you did the work. They are the work.== They're how you think on paper before committing to pixels. Skip them and you design by vibes.

It sounds obvious written down. It wasn't obvious until I lived it. Every project since has built on it: knowing when a project needs an IA map, when it needs an audit, and when it needs neither. Junior me made all of them, all the time, badly. Now I make the ones the project needs, when it needs them.

## The actual design work

![Create Offer form with the plain-language fee breakdown](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/create-offer.png)

The client arrived with references, other crypto marketplaces they wanted us to learn from. Most had information architecture so tangled that "improve on" was the more honest verb.

We took what the references spread across five tabs and brought it down to two or three, with cleaner terminology and controls that appear only when they matter. The fees got their own treatment: a plain-language breakdown of what you're paying and why. Not "Gas: 0.0023 ETH" with no context, but "you're paying X to make the trade and Y to move the token to your wallet, Z in total."

==Small changes, big trust impact.== The kind of work that doesn't look impressive in a screenshot but matters in someone's hands.

## What I'd do differently

![Edit NFT Key Offer form](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/edit-offer.png)

The work was solid for where I was. But I'd push harder on the contract-skirting feature: ask who actually benefits, and maybe refuse to design it if the answer wasn't good. ==Junior me didn't push back on briefs enough.== Junior me said yes a lot.

I'd also care less about visual polish and more about decision architecture. Which choices does the user really have, and how do we make the consequential ones obvious without shouting about all of them equally?

But the bones hold. We took a domain that defaults to casino and made it look like a real product. The structure was better than what the client came in with, the fees were readable, and the trust signals were deliberate.

## What it taught me

![Mobile My Assets screens](/images/cases/linear-marketplace/mobile-assets.png)

==Working in a domain you don't understand is uncomfortable, then terrifying, then the most useful thing in the world.== You have no priors. You can't fall back on the standard pattern because you don't know it yet. You have to actually look at the problem.

That's a muscle, and I built a lot of it here. Every project since has been easier for it.

I still don't trade crypto. But I know what's broken about it and what fixing it looks like. That part stayed.
